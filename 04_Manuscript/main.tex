\section*{Methods}

\subsection{Experimental data aqcuisition}\label{sec:expdata}
\indent The model implemented in this project aims to explain the mechanism of serotonin's effects during anaesthesia. Particularly, the model is tailored to the recording procedure and findings that were obtained in an experiment designed and conducted by Dr. Guido Meijer at the Champalimaud Foundation in Lisbon, Portugal. In this experiment, neural activity in several brain regions was measured using Neuro\-pixels (electrodes measuring electrophysiological activity), after transient stimula\-tion of the serotonergic neurons in the DRN. For precise and local stimula\-tion, optogenetics \cite{deisseroth2015optogenetics} was used. The experiment was conducted on anaesthetized mice.\\
\indent The following areas were recorded and replicated in the model: superior colliculus (SC), midbrain reticular nucleus (MRN), secondary motor cortex (M2), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), 
orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), piriform cortex (PIR), striatum (STR), Amygdala (Amyg), retrosplenial cortex (RSP),  visual cortex (VIS), hippocampus (Hipp), striatum (Str), olfactory cortex (OLF), and piriform cortex (Pir).

\subsection{Model structure}

\noindent
\indent The model implemented in this project is a bistable firing rate model, consisting of multiple local circuits that all-to-all connected form a global network. The local circuits represent the relevant brain areas from the experimental recordings. Serotonin stimulation is modelled by inducing a current to all regions, originating from the DRN. Furthermore, it includes connectivity data from the Allen Brain Atlas that define the connectivity between the regions in the model as well as the projection density from the DRN to the different regions.  

\subsubsection{Local circuit model \label{sec:lc}}
\noindent
\indent This section describes the local circuit model which is used to simulate the average firing rate in individual brain areas in the model. The local circuit model has to fulfil the condition that it is a dynamical firing rate model, which produces UP and DOWN states similar to the states that could be recorded during anaesthesia \cite{ruiz-mejiasSlowFastRhythms2011}. Therefore, we chose a model proposed by Jercog et al. \cite{jercogUPDOWNCorticalDynamics2017}, which is a simplistic model including one excitatory population $E$ and one inhibitory population $I$. Furthermore, it includes an adaptation module $a$, which simulates synaptic fatigue in pyramidal cells, as well as background noise which is introduced to the E and I population. There are projections implemented between both populations, as well as a self coupling mechanism. 

The local circuit model follows three main equations, as defined in Jercog et al. \cite{jercogUPDOWNCorticalDynamics2017}, which describe the change of the populations $E$ and $I$ firing rate $r$ (see Equation \ref{eq:1} and \ref{eq:2}), as well as the strength of adaptation $a$ (see Equation \ref{eq:3}) over time, where $\tau_E, \tau_I$ and $\tau_a$ are time constants.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:1}
    \tau_E \frac{dr_E}{dt} = -r_E(t) + \varphi_E (J_{EE}r_E(t) - J_{EI}r_I(t)-a(t)+\sigma\xi_E(t))
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:2}
    \tau_I \frac{dr_I}{dt} = -r_I(t) + \varphi_I (J_{IE}r_E(t) - J_{II}r_I(t)+\sigma\xi_I(t))
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:3}
    \tau_a \frac{da}{dt} = -a(t) + \beta r_E(t)
\end{equation}

The frequency of UP and DOWN state changes is defined by $\frac{1}{\tau_a}$, so the original value of ${\tau_a=500ms}$ generates states in the frequen\-cy of around 2Hz. Analyses of the experimental data in the mouse re\-vealed that the frequency of state change is 0.5-1Hz, therefore a value of $\tau_a=1500ms$ was chosen and the other time constants were scaled accordingly, so that for the final imple\-mentation it yields that $\tau_E=30ms$ and $\tau_I=6ms$. 
\\
The variable $J$ defines the local coupling strength for the E and I population, which is $J_EI = 5$, $J_EE = 1$, $J_EE = 10$, and $J_EE = 0.5$ (the first letter indicating the target population).  
\\
\\
\indent To model the noise of the system, which is introduced as a background noise to both populations, two Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes are implemented, with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of $\sigma=3.5$. 
\\
The functions $\varphi_E$ and $\varphi_I$ are called the transfer functions, which determine the reaction of the population to incoming currents, through self coupling, projections coming from other populations, or/and background noise. The equation is a threshold-linear function, with a threshold that defines the start of the linear part of the equation. If the input does not reach the threshold, the activation of the regarding population is set to zero. 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:4}
    \varphi_X(x) = g_X[x-\theta_X]_+\qquad,X=\{E,I\}
\end{equation}

The threshold that has to be reached until the population reacts to incoming input is described by $\theta$. The value that I used for the implementation of brain regions that show UP and DOWN states is $\theta_E = 6$ and $\theta_I = 25$. The choice of those parameters is explained in the following section.
\\
In case the input reaches the threshold level, the slope $g$ determines the output. The $g$ controls how sensitive the population reacts to input. A higher $g$ will lead to a stronger output than a lower $g$. The original values are $g_E = 1Hz$ and $g_I = 4Hz$, which will be manipulated later on, in order to reach firing rates that are more similar to the experimentally measured firing rates. 
\\

\noindent\textbf{UP and DOWN state dynamics}
\\
\indent Several components of the model play a role to generate the UP-DOWN dynamics. Most importantly, the system requires bistability, meaning that the model has two stable states of activity, which are called stable attractor points. When one of those points is reached, the network stays at that rate of activity if there is no perturbing signal from the outside reaching the network. The first attractor point represents the DOWN state, in which no firing rate is observed. The second attractor point represents the UP state, with an average firing rate that depends on the setting of local connectivity parameters (self coupling and coupling between E and I). The bistability of the local circuit is reached by the coupling between E and I population and the way the transfer functions are implemented. To find the two attractor points of the system, refer to the nullclines of $r_E(t)$ and $r_I(t)$. Equation \ref{eq:4} and \ref{eq:5} describes $r_E(t)$ and $r_I(t)$, and when solving the equation with setting $r_E=0$ and $r_I=0$, it yields the nullcline of each population. Under specific conditions the nullclines intersect at two points, which are the two attractor points in which both firing rates stay the same.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:5}
    r_E = g_E[J_EEr_E-J_EIrI-a-\theta_E]_+
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} %\label{eq:5}
    r_I = g_I[J_IEr_E-J_IIrI-\theta_I]_+
\end{equation}

Where the two nullclines intersect depends on the threshold $\theta$ of both populations, since it defines the start of the incline of the linear function. If $\theta_E < \theta_I$, the nullcline of $r_E$ has time to incline linearly, whereas the nullcline of $r_I$ starts rising from zero only later on (see Figure \ref{fig:phase_diagram1} A). The fact that $g_E < g_I$, allows the nullclines to intersect and therefore allowing the stable attractor point for the UP state arise (see Figure \ref{fig:phase_diagram2} A).

The effect of the nullclines' slope on the B) E and C) I populations' firing rate. The purple square indicates the default parameter setting.}
    \label{fig:phase_diagram1}
\end{figure}


Adaptation and the fluctuating noise in both populations can initiate the change between the two attractor points of the system. The threshold of the excitatory population $\theta_E$ and the adaptation strength $\beta$ are two parameters that determine the transitions between one state to the other. Figure \ref{fig:phase_diagram2} C) visualises the change of UP and DOWN state frequencies in a phase diagram of the parameters $\beta$ and $\theta_E$.
\\
\indent The circuit dynamics can be described with a limit cycle regime with oscillatory pattern of $\frac{1}{tau_A} Hz$. The originally defined time constants produce UP and DOWN activity with the frequency of 2 Hz. Since the electrophysiological recordings from the mouse brain showed that the UP and DOWN states have a frequency of 0.5-1 Hz, all time constants in the model were multiplied by 3, which leads to lower frequencies (see Figure \ref{fig:phase_diagram2} C).
\\

\subsubsection{Global circuit\label{sec:gc}}
\noindent
\indent By combining several local circuits, we implement a network of brain areas. The goal is to simulate the brain areas that were measured experimentally (see \ref{sec:expdata} for names of all simulated regions).


\subsubsection{Heterogeneity}
\noindent
\indent The brain regions that are modelled in this project are heterogeneous in terms of firing rate, and characteristics of UP and DOWN states. The electrophysiological measurements show that some regions only show UP states, whereas others show a clear UP-DOWN dynamic. The goal is to incorporate this behaviour in the network model. As we know from the local circuit model, the frequency of the UP and DOWN states can be modified by changing the adaptation strength $\beta$ and the threshold of the E population $\theta_E$. Areas that show UP-DOWN dynamics in the electrophysiological recordings in anaesthetised mice are cortical areas as well as the striatum. A value of $\beta = 6$ and $\theta_E = 4$ is set for those regions, since a strong adaptation value leads to reliable generation of UP and DOWN states. Areas that do not show DOWN states, are midbrain areas as well as the hippocampus. This behaviour is achieved by using a low adaptation value $\beta = 1$ and a slightly higher threshold for E $\theta_E = 5$. Regions that show more unclear UP-DOWN states in the electrophysiological data receive a parameter setting of a medium adaptation value $\beta = 3$ and $\theta_E = 5$.
\\
\indent Besides the state frequency, the average firing rate of the E and I populations in each area can be adapted to match the experimental data. The adjustment is realised by cautiously modifying the slope of each populations transfer function (the $g$ in Equation \ref{eq:4}). This modification of the firing rates in each of the local circuit models (brain areas) so that the average firing rate of the E and I population is slightly below the value measured in the mouse brain, since this value will increase later on due to the communication between the areas. The average firing rate values in the model therefore approximate the measured rates (+-5 spikes per seconds).  
\subsection{Connectivity\label{sec:abc}}
\noindent
\indent The Allen Brain Institute provides an atlas of the mouse brain, including the connectivity between brain regions. This allows for more realistic modelling of the global circuit, and the investigation of the question of how relevant (heterogeneous) projections are.
The Allen Brain Atlas can be accessed by using the API that is provided. The package \verb|allensdk| \cite{fulcherBenfulcherAllenSDKInitial2020} was used for this purpose. The Mouse Connectivity Atlas contains experiments about the projections between brain areas in Cre-modified and normal mice. In each experiment, the researchers injected a retrograde viral tracer into a brain structure. The brain areas that were downloaded from the Mouse Connectivity Atlas were chosen based on the areas identified in the recordings of the experimental study (Meijer et al., in preparation). The areas in the experimental study were defined in terms of the Beryl atlas, which is an atlas that is mapping brain structures with acronyms and parcellations slightly different from the Allen Brain Atlas. The regions defined in Beryl annotations were translated to the appropriate brain regions in the Allen atlas and grouped together to higher level areas. For each area, we obtained the projection density values to all relevant areas. Results from all experiments per region were averaged. Since some experiments do not show any projection values, a threshold of a projection density of 0.005 was applied for the experiment to be included. As a next step, the obtained values of the fine grained areas were grouped together into higher level brain areas that each represent one component of the network (see Figure \ref{fig:connectivity}).
\\
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/Methods/figure1.pdf}
    \caption[Connectivity from the Allen Brain Atlas]{Connectivity from the Allen Brain Atlas. A) shows the long-range projections between areas (x-axis: target regions) and B) the serotonergic projections originating from the DRN.}
    \label{fig:connectivity}
\end{figure}

\indent Besides the connectivity values between the areas within the network, the serotonergic projections originating from the DRN are required to simulate the release of serotonin, since the DRN projects to some regions more than to others. For this, we used experiments in which selectively serotonergic projections are traced. Similar to the connectivity matrix, the results are grouped together into the areas that are relevant in the network model (see Figure \ref{fig:connectivity}). 
\\
\indent To introduce the connectivity data in the global network model, the projection density values $W^{xy}$ (projection to area $x$ from area $y$) are multiplied with the coupling strength $G$ between the inter-area projections. The input into population E of area $x$ can therefore be defined as $I^x_E$ from projections that originate from all other areas y (see Equation \ref{eq:8}). 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:8}
    I^x_E = G \sum_y W^{xy}
\end{equation}



\subsubsection{Modelling Serotonin Stimulation\label{sec:mss}}

\indent 

To simulate the inhibitory effect that serotonin seems to have on most brain areas during anaesthesia, a current that represents the input from serotonergic neurons originating from the DRN projecting to the isolated local circuits is introduced. Similar to the connectivity matrix for the inter-area long-range projections, the projections of the DRN are weighted depending on the projection density. Considering that the DRN projection densities to all areas $x$ are stored in a vector $\vec{D}$ the input current $I_{DRN}$ is defined as the following:


\begin{equation}\label{eq:9}
    I^x_{DRN}(t) = S\vec{D}_x u(t)
\end{equation}

The recorded neurons in the mouse brain react to serotonin release in a trajectory in which there is a peak modulation observed after 0.5 seconds and a decay of this effect until it returns to the original state before optogenetic stimulation after 3 seconds. The time curve of this trajectory, here defined as $u(t)$ (see Figure \ref{fig:serotonin_release}) is multiplied with the parameter for serotonin stimulation strength $S$ and is therefore simulating the gradual serotonin release and delayed decay. The stimulation is applied for 1 second, in an inter-trial interval of 3-6 seconds, simulating transient optogenetic stimulation, mimicking the experimental procedure.
\\
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{img/Methods/serotonin_trajectory.pdf}
    \caption[Serotonin release trajectory]{Serotonin release trajectory $u(t)$. Estimated based on experimental data. A serotonin release of 1 indicates the full possible capacity of its release.}
    \label{fig:serotonin_release} 
\end{figure}

Now, the target of the serotonin stimulation $I_{DRN}$ in the model is considered. The transition from an UP towards a DOWN state is influenced by the threshold $\theta_E$ and the E populations' capability of reaching this threshold. Lowering the input to the E population makes it more difficult for the E population to reach $\theta_E$. At the same time, as the adaptation towards E increases, it makes it even harder to reach the threshold and the system can eventually not stay in an UP state anymore, and therefore transitions into a DOWN state. By introducing an inhibitory current to the E population (subtracting  $I_{DRN}$ from $r_E$), it becomes harder to cross the threshold and it is more likely that the system eventually transitions into a DOWN state, which is exactly the effect that serotonin seems to have on the brain network. 
\\

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{img/Methods/GlobalModel.pdf}
    \caption[Global network model]{Global network model. The implemented model comprises 14 areas (3 example regions are displayed), which are interconnected.}
    \label{fig:connect} 
\end{figure}

The described global model is extending the local circuit model proposed by Jercog et al. \cite{jercogUPDOWNCorticalDynamics2017}. The dynamics of the global model are formulated in Equation \ref{eq:6} (for the E population) and Equation \ref{eq:7} (for the I population). The formula for the adaptation remains as described in Equation \ref{eq:3}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:6}
\tau \frac{dr_E}{dt} = - r_E(t) J_{EE} r_E(t) - J_{EI} r_I(t) - r_A(t) + \theta_E + \sigma \xi_X(t) + I_E - I_{DRN}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:7}
        \tau \frac{dr_I}{dt} = -r_I(t) J_{II} r_I(t) - J_{IE} r_E(t) + \theta_I + \sigma \xi_X(t) \\[20pt]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Model modifications}
\noindent
\indent To examine which components of the model are crucial for the replication of serotonin's effect, a subset of the simulations are run with a slightly modified model. 
\subsection{Modified connectivity}
\noindent
\indent The goal is to shine light on the role of connectivity in the brain on the effect of serotonin, and therefore understand the importance of communication between the different areas. For this purpose, two aspects are examined: 1) the strength, and 2) the heterogeneity of the coupling. The coupling strength was modified by changing the value of $G$. To test the relevance of heterogeneous coupling, the connectivity matrix was replaced by a unique value for all brain areas. To ensure that this homogeneous connectivity is not too strong or too weak, the value is calculated from the mean of the connectivity matrix, so that $W$ is replaced by \(\bar{W} = \frac{1}{n} \sum W\).

\subsection{Modulating the mechanism of modelling serotonin}
\noindent
\indent Intuitively, adding a current to the I populations rate should lead to the same effect, as it shifts the I nullcline to the left, such that the attractor point representing the UP state (intersection of the two nullclines) eventually disappears and the system would transition into a DOWN state. 
To validate the assumption and determine if stimulation can induce DOWN states through alternative means, simulations in which population I is targeted by an excitatory current were run. This is achieved by adding 
$I_{DRN}$ to $r_I$ instead of subtracting $I_{DRN}$ from $r_E$.
\\
\indent Given the potential for unexpected results in exploring the different mechanisms of serotonin stimulation, additional simulations were performed by targeting the E population with an excitatory current (reverting the sign of $I_{DRN}$ in Equation \ref{eq:6}) and the I population with an inhibitory current (subtracting $I_{DRN}$ from $r_I$). These simulations aim to determine if these mechanisms would also lead to a comparable effect and to evaluate the significance of the chosen approach for modelling serotonin stimulation in achieving the observed effects in the experimental data.

\section{Analysis methodologies}

\subsubsection{Hidden Markov Model for state classification\label{sec:hmm}}
\noindent
\indent To analyse the UP and DOWN states in the simulated activity, a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) was applied. We used the package \verb|ssm| developed by Linderman et al. \cite{Linderman_SSM_Bayesian_Learning_2020}, which implements state classification with HMMs using Bayesian inference. To classify the states using HMMs, first, a HMM model has to be defined, and the number of possible states has to be specified. Then, the HMM is fit to the firing rate data. Here, we used the I population's firing rate, since it is easier to classify based on its high firing rate relative to the E population's firing rate. It is now possible to predict the most likely state for every time step. Based on the HMM state classification, analyses of the probability of a certain state at a given time are possible.

\subsection{Synchrony in the global network model}\label{cha:brunel}
\noindent
\indent Previous evidence showed that inter-area communication introduces synchrony within a network \cite{varelaBrainwebPhaseSynchronization2001, holcmanEmergenceStatesCortical2006}. This might also be the case of the UP-DOWN dynamics that occur in each area. For example, if an UP state is induced in one area, the elevated firing rate is propagated to the E population of the other areas, increasing the chance of a simultaneous transition into an UP state in the targeted areas. To measure the synchrony of the areas in the network, we computed the parameter $\chi(N)$ proposed by Brunel \cite{brunelCommunicatedPaulTiesinga2006} (see Equation \ref{eq:brunel1} and \ref{eq:brunel2}). The idea behind the formula is to take the variance
oan each isolated area and compare it to the variance of the entire network. $N$ denotes the number of local areas in the network, and r is the I population's firing rate.  

\begin{equation}\label{eq:brunel1}
    \sigma^2_r = \langle [r(t)]^2  \rangle_t - [
 \langle r(t) \rangle_t]^2 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:brunel2}
    \chi(N) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2_x}{\frac{1}{N}\sum^N_(i=1)\sigma^2_{r_i}}}
\end{equation}

This formula will provide us with information about the synchrony of every region compared to the global synchrony.

\section*{Results}

\noindent
\indent To investigate the mechanism behind the effect of serotonin stimulation in mice during anaesthesia, a dynamic firing rate model was implemented, which simulates a simplified network of the mouse brain. Each brain area was modelled using a local excitatory-inhibitory (EI) circuit consisting of one E, one I population, and a mechanism controlling for adaptation (A) of the excitatory population. The implementation was guided by the EI network model proposed by Jercog et al. \cite{jercogUPDOWNCorticalDynamics2017}, who showed that this model produces UP and DOWN states similar to the UP and DOWN states that were observed in recordings from mice during anaesthesia \cite{ruiz-mejiasSlowFastRhythms2011}.

\section{Replication of mouse brain dynamics in an anaesthetic state\label{sec:prereq}}

\subsubsection{UP and DOWN states}
\noindent
\indent The model was able to reproduce the slow-wave oscillations that were observed during anaesthesia in previous experiments. Figure \ref{fig:UPDOWNstates} shows the results of a simulated session using the connected network model. Certain areas showed periods of high (UP) and low (DOWN) activity in the firing rate of the E and I populations. The frequency of the UP and DOWN states cycle was around 0.5-1Hz, which simulates the frequency of the UP-DOWN dynamics observed in the experimental data (Meijer et al., in preparation). The connections between the 14 areas on the figure were formed with a coupling strength of $G=2$, to ensure that the UP-DOWN dynamics were synchronous across regions (more about synchrony in section \ref{sec:synchronyresults} and Figure \ref{fig:synchrony}). The slow-wave oscillations occurred without any serotonin interference, as this simulation did not include the stimulation by serotonin.

\subsubsection{Heterogeneity in the global circuit}\label{sec:heterogresults}

\noindent
\indent In the electrophysiological recordings it was observed that the UP-DOWN dynamics differed across brain regions. The hippocampus and midbrain areas clearly showed no state activity patterns, whereas cortical areas and the striatum clearly showed UP and DOWN states. The amygdala, thalamus and piriform showed less clear UP and DOWN states.
Adjusting the theta and beta parameter allowed for modification of the frequency of the UP-DOWN dynamics. This enabled introducing heterogeneity in the global network model making the simulation as close as possible to the experimental data. For example, in Figure \ref{fig:UPDOWNstates} the hippocampus only showed an UP state for the entire duration of the model simulation. This replicates the findings from the electrophysiological data and could be reached by setting the adaptation value to $\beta = 0$ and $\theta_E = 5$.

\subsubsection{Synchrony in the connected global network}\label{sec:synchronyresults}
\noindent
\indent The UP-DOWN dynamics that were visible during anaesthesia occurred in a synchronous rhythmic pattern. To evaluate the amount of synchrony in the network model, a measure proposed by Brunel and Hansel \cite{brunelCommunicatedPaulTiesinga2006} was applied (see Section \ref{cha:brunel} for methodological details). Running the model simulation using different coupling strength values tests
if the model follows this mechanism. Increasing the coupling strength increased the synchrony of the firing rates across the regions of the network. Since some of the areas did not show any UP and DOWN states, full synchrony ($\chi=1$) is not possible to reach. If not otherwise specified, $G=2$ was used in the following stimulation, corresponding to a value of $\chi=0.55$ enabling enough synchrony in the system without entirely eliminating region-specific activity patterns. The effect of different coupling strengths on the effect of serotonin is evaluated in section \ref{sec:effcoupling}.
\\
\indent In summary, the previous simulations using the global circuit model showed that the implemented model is capable of simulating brain activity during anaesthesia, which makes it a good tool to investigate serotonin's effects on UP-DOWN dynamics in a large-scale network.


\subsection{Replicating serotonin's effect on UP-DOWN dynamics\label{sec:sereffect}}
\noindent
\indent Optogenetic stimulating of the DRN in mice during anaesthesia led to three main observations. In certain areas serotonin-induced 1) a DOWN state (strong effect in the frontal cortex, amygdala and striatum, weaker effect in sensory cortex, hippocampus, and thalamus), 2) a rebound UP state post-stimulation, and 3) a delayed onset of the response to the serotonin stimulation.
\\
\indent In the proposed large-scale model, serotonin stimulation was modelled by targeting the E population with an inhibitory current. Here, a serotonin stimulation of strength $S=40$ was applied. In Figure \ref{fig:downstate_repl}, the effect of serotonin in the model and in the experimental data are compared (the top panel represents the model results, and the lower panel represents the results from electrophysiological recordings).
\\
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{img/Results/Figure2.pdf}
    \caption[Mouse vs. Model]{Mouse vs. Model. Serotonin stimulation induced a DOWN state in several regions. The top graph shows the model simulation, whereas the plot below demonstrates the findings from the recordings in mice. The y-axis shows the $\Delta$ DOWN state probability, which expresses the difference between the probability of a DOWN state occurring in stimulation sessions compared to sessions without stimulation (baseline).}
    \label{fig:downstate_repl}
\end{figure}
\\
\indent It was clearly visible that during optogenetic stimulation of the DRN in mice (area highlighted in light blue), the probability of a DOWN state increased between 25-50\% in the frontal cortex, amygdala and striatum, compared to the baseline activity, in which no stimulation was applied. The sensory cortex, hippocampus and thalamus showed an induced DOWN state too, though slightly less than the previously named areas (10-12\%). The midbrain seemed not to be affected by the serotonin modulation. With simulations using the proposed model very similar effects can be observed. In the frontal cortex, striatum, sensory cortex, and thalamus the probability increased by about 50\% in comparison with the baseline, and the amygdala showed an increase of even around 90\%. Note that the DOWN state probability in the striatum was not significantly different from zero (see \ref{fig:downstate_barplot}), whereas this was the case in the other named regions. The midbrain shows a slight increase (significantly different from the baseline mean) of the DOWN state probability, different to the recordings in mice, where the midbrain was not affected by the stimulation. What is interesting is that the hippocampus did not seem to be modulated by serotonin in the model, despite the observed 10\% increased probability of a DOWN state in the mouse brain. 
\\
\indent Some regions indicated the return of an UP state after the induced DOWN state in the mouse data, which are the frontal cortex, sensory cortex (though very marginal), and thalamus. In the model it is striking that the rebound UP state were slightly stronger expressed than in the mouse data; for example, the frontal cortex, sensory cortex and thalamus – overlapping with the affected regions in the experimental data -, and additionally in the striatum showed a rebound UP state. In the model, the rebound UP state was induced much faster than in the experimental condition. 


\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{img/Results/downstate_replicationS40_G[2].pdf}
    \caption[Effect of serotonin on DOWN state probability]{Effect of serotonin on DOWN state probability. Serotonin stimulation induces a DOWN state in several regions. Probability values between 0.5-1 seconds after the serotonin stimulation onset are included.}
    \label{fig:downstate_barplot}
\end{figure}

\indent What stands out is that the striatum showed strong fluctuations in the model simulations, in comparison to the other regions and in comparison to the measurements in mice.
In all recorded areas displaying a modulatory effect, there was a delay of 0.5 seconds in inducing a DOWN state. This delay was simulated by implementing the time course of the serotonergic response. 
Overall, the main effect of serotonin that was observed in the electrophysiological recordings of mice could be replicated with minimal exceptions.


\subsection{Effects of coupling strength\label{sec:effcoupling}}
\noindent
\indent In this section, the potential underlying mechanisms behind serotonergic modulation will be addressed. One aspect to consider is the connectivity between the regions in the network, which are crucial for inter-area communication. The coupling strength of those inter-area projections was modified by increasing the value of the parameter $G$ that defines the weight of the connectivity matrix $W$. If the effect of serotonin on the UP and DOWN dynamics acts in a direct way, inter-area coupling would not be required to replicate the induced DOWN state. Though, if the successful replication of the effect requires inter-area coupling, serotonin acts indirectly. This mechanism might vary across different areas. We applied serotonin stimulation and systematically increased the coupling strength $G$, to gain insights into the role of inter-area coupling in controlling the effect of serotonin stimulation. 
\\
\indent In an unconnected network (G=0), the probability of a DOWN state (compared to baseline activity) is dependent on the strength of the DRN projections targeting each area (see Figure \ref{fig:couplingstrength} A). Regions to which the DRN strongly connects, showed a strong DOWN state probability during serotonin stimulation.  The amygdala for example receives very strong connections from the DRN and therefore showed the highest modulatory effect of all of the regions in an unconnected network. The visual cortex, on the other hand, receives fewer projections from the DRN and is therefore almost not affected by the simulated serotonin stimulation. 
\\


\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{img/Results/couplingstrength_trajectories_S40.pdf}
    \caption[Effect of coupling strength - Example traces]{Effect of coupling strength, demonstrated in example regions. The blue window indicates serotonin stimulation. The differently coloured graphs display different coupling strengths $G$ from $0-3$. The demonstrated example regions are the visual cortex (VIS), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), thalamus (Thal), and amygdala (Amyg).}
    \label{fig:couplingstrength_trajectories}
\end{figure}
\indent To investigate the effect of coupling strength, simulations with various coupling strengths were run ($G$ of $0-3$). Figure \ref{fig:couplingstrength_trajectories} demonstrates the resulting traces of DOWN state probability (each compared to the respective baseline) for the different coupling strength values in the visual cortex, medial prefrontal cortex, thalamus and amygdala. Modifying the coupling strength did not have the same effect in all areas. Whereas in the visual cortex, an increased coupling strength increased the DOWN state probability, the amygdala showed no big difference in DOWN state probability during serotonin stimulation for all G parameters. Additionally, it is noticeable that for $G=1$ in the visual cortex and the mPFC, the rebound UP state is distinctively pronounced.
In an unconnected network, the visual cortex was only slightly affected by serotonin stimulation (see Figure \ref{fig:couplingstrength} B). It was observed that with an increased coupling strength, the probability of a DOWN state increased in the visual cortex; it was around 50\% higher in the case of strong inter-area connections compared to no coupling.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{img/Results/Figure3.pdf}
    \caption[Effect of coupling strength]{Effect of coupling strength. In all plots, DOWN state probabilities in a time window of 0.5-1 seconds post-stimulation were taken into account. A) In an unconnected network (G=0), the DRN projection density determines the strength of the effect of the modulation by serotonin. B) shows that the serotonin effect was elevated in the visual cortex for increased coupling strength (p<0.05). In C), the difference between the $\Delta$ DOWN state probability for a coupling strength G=0 and G=3 is displayed for every region. The colour codes describe the average projection densities that each area receives from all other areas, indicating how strongly the areas are affected by the changes in coupling strength.}
    \label{fig:couplingstrength}
\end{figure}
\\
\indent To uncover if this effect was similar for all of the regions, the difference between $\Delta$ DOWN state probabilities in an unconnected network and a strongly connected network were investigated (see Figure \ref{fig:couplingstrength} C). It turns out that whereas some areas were strongly affected by the change in coupling strength, others were not. The difference (G=0 vs G=3) was high for the visual cortex (p<0.05), but close to zero for the amygdala. Areas that did not show any UP-DOWN states were not affected by the increase in coupling strength either.
\\
\indent This could not be explained by the strength of DRN connectivity, as the negative correlation was non-significant (r=-0.27, p=0.36). The projection densities (obtained from the Allan Brain Atlas) describing the connection strength between the areas co-existing in this network vary (see legend in Figure \ref{fig:couplingstrength} C) and might affect how each area reacts to the coupling strength. The visual cortex receives relatively rich projections from other areas in the network, compared to the midbrain, striatum, thalamus, hippocampus, secondary motor cortex, and the remaining sensory regions. The thalamus showed, despite strong incoming inter-area projections, no enhanced serotonin effect for an increased coupling strength.   
\\
\indent Overall, the coupling strength affected serotonin's induced DOWN state in some but not all areas, which could not be explained by the strength of DRN projections. Neither was the density of inter-area projections of each area a determining factor. Therefore, it is interesting to examine the heterogeneous connectivity pattern of the regions in this network model.

\subsection{Heterogeneous connectivity}\label{sec:heterogconnect}
\noindent
\indent The previous results showed that the area specific connectivity pattern seems to be relevant for achieving an effect in some areas (see above in Section \ref{sec:effcoupling}). Using information about the projection densities between areas (from the Allen Brain Atlas) implemented the area specific connectivity in the model and improved its biological plausibility. To investigate the relevance and impact of this effort on the obtained results, the simulations using the 'realistic' connectivity values were compared to simulations using homogeneous connectivity values, in which the mean of the entire connectivity matrix was applied as a single weight for all inter-area projections. 


\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{img/Results/Figure5.pdf}
    \caption[Heterogeneous vs. homogeneous connectivity]{Heterogeneous vs. homogeneous connectivity. A) shows the trajectories of the DOWN state probability in homogeneous and heterogeneous connectivity compared to each of the baselines (coupling strength of $G=2$). B) demonstrates the difference between homogeneous and heterogeneous connectivity seen in A). Regions that did not show any UP and DOWN areas were excluded. The regions on the x-axis are sorted by incoming projection strength (from all other areas) from high density to low density. In C), the difference in $\Delta$ DOWN state probability between $G=0$ and $G=3$ in the homogeneous condition is plotted and correlated with the DRN projection strength of each area. B) and C) include data in a time window of 0.5-1 seconds post-stimulation.}
    \label{fig:heterogenconnect}
\end{figure}
In the homogeneous condition, a DOWN state was induced upon serotonin stimulation in all areas (Figure \ref{fig:heterogenconnect} A). In some regions, the heterogeneous coupling mechanism strengthened the effect of serotonin in comparison to the homogeneous coupling, whereas in other regions the effect was reduced (see Figure \ref{fig:heterogenconnect} B). The thalamus for example, which receives strong projections from the other areas in the heterogeneous condition, did not show a great difference between the homogeneous and heterogeneous condition. The piriform cortex, which is an area that does not receive many projections from other areas, showed an increased effect of serotonin is higher in the heterogeneous condition. Another example is the secondary motor cortex, whose response did not change a lot in the homogeneous coupling condition even though it also receives few projections in the heterogeneous condition. These different results in the homogeneous and heterogeneous conditions might be created due to the specific areas from which both regions receive input, as well as the strength of the DRN projections that both areas received. In the heterogeneous condition, the piriform cortex received projections mainly from the olfactory cortex, which was strongly targeted by the DRN. This connectivity profile was overwritten by the homogeneous connectivity value. 
\\
\indent In the homogeneous condition, there was a significant negative correlation (p<0.01) found between the projection density originating from the DRN and the difference in serotonin-induced DOWN state of a coupling strength of $G=0$ versus $G=3$ (see Figure \ref{fig:heterogenconnect} C). The increased coupling strength ($G=3$) had a higher influence on regions with low DRN projection densities. In some regions with a higher DRN connectivity, strong homogeneous coupling even reduced the DOWN state probability (e.g., in the amygdala).
\\
\indent In summary, heterogeneous inter-area coupling increased the efficacy by which of serotonin-induced DOWN states, compared to homogeneous coupling. Homogeneous coupling led to an evenly distributed effect across the regions.

\section{The mechanism of serotonin stimulation}
\noindent
\indent The mechanism used to simulate serotonin in the model plays a crucial role in replicating the effects observed in the experimental data. Here, the consequences of modifying the approach to serotonin stimulation within the model were investigated. Specifically, it was chosen to model serotonin stimulation as an inhibitory current targeting the E population ('$E-$'), as this increases the threshold for the E population to fire, making it likely to induce a transition from the UP to the DOWN state. It is also intuitive to consider the opposite operation of targeting the I population with an excitatory current ('$I+$'), as it may yield similar results. Figure \ref{fig:targetI} illustrates the outcomes of the simulation under this condition. 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{img/Results/Figure4_1.pdf}
    \caption[Modelling serotonin by targeting the I population with an excitatory current]{Modelling serotonin by targeting the I population with an excitatory current. A) $\Delta$ DOWN state probability in all simulated brain regions. B) Difference between targeting E with an excitatory current and targeting I with an inhibitory current.}
    \label{fig:targetI}
\end{figure}


The graph reveals that, in many regions such as the Amygdala, cortical areas (OLF, OFC, M2, Vis, Pir, and RSP) and the striatum, a first spike of an induced DOWN state took place, followed by an UP state at around 1 second after stimulation onset. This is interesting since this is the opposite effect of what was observed when targeting the E population with an inhibitory current, as the main effect, the induced DOWN state could be observed around the same time as the UP state in this modified serotonin stimulation. Note, that the highest $\Delta$ DOWN state probability did not exceed 30\%, which is very low in comparison to the probabilities found when targeting population E with an inhibitory current (see Figure \ref{fig:downstate_repl}). In the somatosensory cortex as well as in the piriform cortex and thalamus, the initial DOWN state that occurred within the first 0.5 seconds after stimulation was longer and less 'spiky' that in the other regions named above. The serotonin stimulation acts gradually, therefore the current applied to I is rather weak at first. Nevertheless, the local circuit reacted to the weak current applied to the I population with an increased DOWN state probability. In turn, a stronger current had the opposite effect and let the system transition into an UP state. 
\\
\indent Figure \ref{fig:targetI} B shows the direct comparison of the two distinct mechanisms ('$E-$' and '$I+$'). If both mechanisms were to induce the same effect, it was expected to observe a line centred around 0. Though, it is visible that the clear DOWN state in all regions except the hippocampus and the midbrain could not be replicated by the $I+$ mechanism.


\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/Results/Figure4.pdf}
    \caption[Target mechanisms of serotonin stimulation]{Target mechanisms of serotonin stimulation. A) All four options of modelling serotonin stimulation, averaged over all regions in the network. The first letter in the legend description is the population targeted by serotonin, the sign after it indicates the charge direction of the current to that population. B) shows a more detailed view of the 'I-' simulation. Downstate probabilities in a time window of 0.5-1 seconds post-stimulation are taken into account.}
    \label{fig:targetI2}
\end{figure}
\indent To uncover the effects of other options for modelling serotonin stimulation, targeting the E population with an excitatory current ('$E+$') and targeting the I population with an inhibitory current were investigated ('$I-$'). The results of all four mechanisms ('$I+$', '$E-$', '$I-$', and '$E+$') are presented in Figure \ref{fig:targetI2} A. It is evident that only the '$E-$' implementation successfully reproduced a stable induction of a DOWN state during serotonin stimulation, followed by a somewhat less probable rebound UP state. As demonstrated previously, modelling the stimulation using the '$I+$' mechanism failed to replicate the desired effect. Notably, the '$E+$' and the '$I-$' mechanism led to the induction of an UP state with a probability of approximately 10-20\% compared to the baseline (note that the inverse of the DOWN state probability equals the probability of an UP state). Figure \ref{fig:targetI2} B shows that for the '$I-$' mechanism an UP state in frontal areas, amygdala, sensory areas and the thalamus was induced. Interestingly, a subtle induction of a DOWN state was observed in the striatum. This phenomenon could be attributed to the comparatively higher frequency of oscillations in the striatum relative to other areas.
\\
\indent The clear distinctions in the outcomes achieved by each mechanism highlight the complexity of the network model.


\section*{Discussion}

\noindent
\indent Insights from electrophysiological recordings from anaesthetized mice during optogenetic stimulation of the DRN showed that serotonin stimulation induces a DOWN state, mainly in frontal and sensory cortices as well as in the amygdala and striatum. These findings were the basis for the implementation of a firing rate model that simulates the brain activity of the measured regions and aims to uncover the mechanisms of serotonin modulation on oscillatory neural activity during anaesthesia. 
\\
\indent The model used in this project is based on a previously proposed model \cite{jercogUPDOWNCorticalDynamics2017} and extends it by connecting multiple neural curcuits to one comprehensive brain network to investigate the mechanisms of serotonin on UP and DOWN states in anesthesia, which has not been done before. Most studies investigating the serotonin's modulation of oscillations focus on a specific brain region (see \cite{puigSerotoninModulatesFastSpiking2010, puigSerotoninModulationPrefrontoHippocampal2015}). This project implements an approach that investigates serotonin's effect on a large scale and therefore provides exciting insights into its mechanism in the entire brain. 

\section{Replication of serotonin's effect}
\noindent
\indent The simulations performed by the proposed model demonstrate that a DOWN state was induced in the frontal cortex, amygdala, sensory cortex, thalamus, and midbrain, mostly overlapping with the effect observed in the electrophysiological recordings. Notably, no DOWN state could be induced in the hippocampus. This might be due to the fact that the hippocampus is targeted by very few serotonergic projections according to the Allen Brain Atlas. Additionally, it does not receive strong connections from any of the other areas, which could spread the effect of serotonin. In the real brain, there are many more regions that might act as a relay station for information reaching the hippocampus, but since the model only comprises selected areas, this information transfer cannot be taken into account. Another reason might be the parameter setting of the model, determining the emergence UP and DOWN states in each area. It is difficult to determine these dynamics from the electrophysiological data, which is why the parameters are an estimation that might introduce differences between the model simulations and the recorded data. 

\section{Direct and indirect effects of serotonin\label{sec:indirecteffects}}
\noindent
\indent Without coupling between the areas, the effect of serotonin depends on the DRN projections targeting each area (see Figure \ref{fig:couplingstrength} A), which is a direct effect induced by serotonergic neurons targeting each area. Introducing connections between areas and increasing this coupling strength results in an increase in the effect of serotonin in certain brain areas, for example in the visual cortex (see Figure \ref{fig:couplingstrength} B). 
This indirect effect could not be observed in all areas, but which areas do show it does not depend on their initial serotonin effect, and also the density of incoming projections is not decisive (see Figure \ref{fig:couplingstrength} C). The indirect effect of serotonin rather depends on the area specific connectivity pattern. The visual cortex, for example, does not receive strong projections from the DRN but receives many projections from other areas (such as the thalamus and the mPFC) that are strongly targeted by the DRN. For a high coupling strength (e.g., $G=2$), the effect of serotonin can be communicated via the long-range projections, inducing a stronger DOWN state probability in the visual cortex upon serotonin stimulation compared to the effect observed when no coupling between areas exists.
\\
\indent The amygdala receives around the same density of projections from other areas (non-serotonergic) compared to the visual cortex, and still does not show an indirect effect with higher coupling values. The absence of this increased effect in the amygdala may be attributed to its connectivity profile. The amygdala receives robust projections from the DRN, rendering it significantly affected by serotonin stimulation even in the absence of long-range projections between areas. Areas showing a moderate DOWN state during serotonin stimulation provide relatively weak input to the amygdala, and therefore their effect does not substantially influence serotonin's modulation in this region. The thalamus serves as another example; it receives strong incoming projections from other areas, which would suggest the possibility of an indirect serotonin effect. However, no increased DOWN state probability is observed with increased coupling strength. Looking at the connectivity matrix, the visual cortex, which is minimally affected by serotonin release, is the area that targets the thalamus the strongest in this model. The visual cortex and other weak serotonergic target regions are seemingly not able to amplify serotonin’s effect in the thalamus.
\\
\indent It is an important observation that increased coupling strength increases the synchrony in the system. As the weight between the areas strengthens, areas with a strong influence on others communicate their UP and DOWN state transitions to other regions, leading to simultaneous alternation between UP and DOWN states. Additionally, due to the increase in synchrony, the areas communicate induced DOWN states to other areas, amplifying serotonin's effect in certain brain regions. This is in line with previous findings from experiments as well as computational simulations, showing that inter-area communication gives rise to synchrony in the network and communicates incoming currents \cite{varelaBrainwebPhaseSynchronization2001, roelfsema1997visuomotor}. Synchrony is the basis for many cognitive functions like memory consolidation, learning, and perception. Evidence suggests that serotonin influences synchrony in a network \cite{puigSerotoninModulationPrefrontoHippocampal2015}. Though, in this project, serotonin did not lead to desynchronisation of the network but communicated the effect of serotonin to other areas. 
\\
\indent In conclusion, these findings suggest that the "indirect effect" of serotonin is facilitated by the global synchrony in the network and depends on the specific connectivity profile of each region.

\subsection{Realistic connectivity}
\noindent
\indent Evaluating the influence of heterogeneous inter-area coupling on the effect of serotonin on UP and DOWN states, the connectivity values in the connectivity matrix were replaced with the mean value of the projection densities stored in the matrix. 
\\
\indent Under homogeneous coupling, the impact of serotonin is diminished in multiple regions compared to when the connectivity matrix from the Allen Brain atlas is used. In some regions to be closer to the experimental data, for example in the amygdala, the effect is not as strongly pronounced as compared to in the model simulations. However, it is important to note that direct comparisons are complicated since the model does not include all the regions and connections present in the brain. Nevertheless, these findings provide insights into how heterogeneous coupling affects the distribution of serotonin’s effect. Notably, regions with fewer projections do not display an enhanced serotonin effect in the heterogeneous condition compared to the homogeneous condition. On the other hand, stronger connected regions show an increased serotonin effect in the heterogeneous condition (e.g., the piriform cortex). However, this does not hold for all regions; for example, the secondary motor cortex, which despite being strongly targeted by other regions is not strongly influenced by the coupling method. These observations indicate that the individual projection profile of a region plays a role in determining the effect, similar to the finding in the investigations regarding the coupling strength. 
%In some regions, the stronger homogeneous coupling even reduces the effect (e.g., in the amygdala), which are those regions that receive stronger projections from the DRN. In the homogeneous coupling condition, areas are influenced by all other areas equally strongly. Since there are many areas that are weakly connected to the DRN, the few areas on which serotonergic neurons have more influence, are overshadowed by them.
\\
\indent In summary, heterogeneous coupling enhances the serotonin effect, while homogeneous coupling leads to more uniform DOWN state probability trajectories with reduced region-specific characteristics.


\subsection{Only inhibiting excitatory populations induces DOWN states\label{sec:sertargets}}
\noindent 
\indent The way how serotonin is simulated in the model is a crucial step in reproducing the effects that were found in the experimental data. It is known from Jercog et al. \cite{jercogUPDOWNCorticalDynamics2017}, that increasing the threshold of the E population induces the transition to a DOWN state. Adding an inhibitory current to the E population has the same effect since the E population will require more positive input to counteract the inhibitory input to reach the threshold $\theta_E$. Therefore it was expected that when simulating the serotonin stimulation by an inhibitory current, targeting the E population would induce a DOWN state in the system. Intuitively, the symmetric way of doing so - stimulating the I population with an excitatory current - should lead to a similar effect. Instead of the expected effect, exciting I lead to an UP state in all areas of the network. This phenomenon could potentially be attributed to the localised nature of inhibitory connectivity. Inhibitory neurons are only communicating with neurons in their local circuit, in contrast to excitatory neurons (e.g., pyramidal cells), which form excitatory connections beyond the immediate vicinity of their local area and thereby establish long-range projections with other regions in the network. Consequently, within a coupled network, the E population experiences a substantially greater influence compared to the I population, even when subjected to identical serotonin stimulation. To test if the long-range connections really are the determining factor in the failure of the '$I+$' mechanism, simulations without coupling should be analysed in future research.
\\
\\
\indent In light of this counter-intuitive discovery, it is relevant to explore alternative approaches to modelling serotonin stimulation, to investigate if those might unexpectedly lead to similar effects. All possible implementation options are either targeting the E population with an inhibitory, or excitatory current or targeting the I population with an excitatory current. 

\indent The results show that only the '$E-$' mechanism can replicate the effect of inducing a DOWN state (see Figure \ref{fig:targetI2} A). All three other implementations reveal a more or less strongly induced UP state upon stimulation. 

\subsubsection{Paradoxical effect}

\noindent 
\indent Inhibiting the inhibitory population unexpectedly induces a decrease in DOWN state probability in the I population as well as in the E population. The inhibition of the I population might not be sufficient to silence the area. On the contrary, it increases the chance that the population keeps firing and staying or transitioning into an UP state. This might be because of the interconnectivity in the local circuit since the interneuron inhibition decreases the inhibition of the excitatory population. This paradoxical effect has been observed in computational neural circuits and tested in vivo \cite{tsodyksParadoxicalEffectsExternal1997a}. In a biologically inspired circuit model, implemented by Garcia Del Molino et al. \cite{garciadelmolinoParadoxicalResponseReversal2017}, a similar response has been found. The model consists of one pyramidal cell and three interneuron types. Externally inhibiting one of the interneuron populations paradoxically induced an excitatory effect in the same population. For the paradoxical response to emerge, a prerequisite is that the excitatory population possesses sufficient recurrent connections\cite{garciadelmolinoParadoxicalResponseReversal2017, tsodyksParadoxicalEffectsExternal1997a}. Whether that is also the case in this finding, in future research the connectivity in the local circuit should be manipulated systematically. Furthermore, it should be noted that the local circuits within the network implemented in this project all possess identical levels of local connectivity strength, whereas, in reality, this connectivity is likely to vary.
\\
\indent Relating this finding to serotonin's mechanisms, this counter-intuitive response might potentially be realised by the inhibitory effect of the 5HT\textsubscript{1A} receptors. These receptors have an inhibitory effect and are situated on interneurons, thereby leading to their inhibition.
\\
\indent This finding highlights the complexity that can be observed even in simplistic neural circuits, which may be used by serotonin. Serotonin is able to switch its effect in different brain states, such as distinct behavioural states \cite{clarkeCognitiveInflexibilityPrefrontal}. This richness in modulating neural activity underlines the neurotransmitter's powerful role in regulating brain functions.


\section{Methodological Limitations and Advantages}
\noindent
\indent 
Since the model is built only in a limited biologically plausible way, it is not possible to draw conclusions about the role of different receptor types in the modulation of UP and DOWN states during anaesthesia. The fact that only inhibiting the E population could induce the observed DOWN state, might involve both, the inhibitory receptor 5-HT\textsubscript{1A} and the excitatory receptor 5-HT\textsubscript{2A}, as both receptors are located on both inhibitory interneurons as well as excitatory pyramidal neurons pre- and postsynaptically\cite{celadaSerotoninModulationCortical2013}. To uncover the interactive mechanism of the two receptor types, a more fine grained model including different receptor types must be built. For example, it is very likely that the mechanism differs across the regions. For example, because 5-HT\textsubscript{1A} are rich in pyramidal cells axon hillock and soma in the prefrontal cortex, the dominantly inhibitory effect of serotonin in this region may be mostly mediated by 5-HT\textsubscript{1A} \cite{puigSerotoninModulationPrefrontoHippocampal2015}, which might differ from how serotonin affects other regions. Implementing a network with single neurons and even different receptors would provide more insight into how exactly serotonin modulation works.
Additionally, one possible optimisation in terms of biological plausibility would be to implement projections back to the DRN. Evidence shows that there are projections from many brain regions (e.g., from the prefrontal cortex) that regulate the release of serotonin \cite{celadaSerotoninModulationCortical2013}. 
Furthermore, the distance between the nodes in the real brain has not been taken into account in our model. Though, it has been shown that despite the distance, a phase lock of zero time lag between far away regions is possible \cite{vicenteDynamicalRelayingCan2008}. 
\\
\indent Although there are many possibilities to extend the model implemented for this project, the model presented here is still well suited to address questions regarding the relevance of connectivity between network components and the functioning of serotonin on a larger scale. By avoiding an excessively specific implementation and keeping the number of variables low, the model offers greater interpretability. Additionally, the availability of state-of-the-art atlas data does not yet provide the necessary level of detail regarding the distribution of 5-HT receptor types among specific cell types. Moreover, developing more detailed models would significantly increase the computational complexity of simulations and analyses. Taking these factors into account, the proposed model strikes a balance that allows sufficient biological plausibility for the questions raised to allow meaningful investigation.
